# Unit 7: Exponents & Roots (지수와 근)

## 7.1 Powers and Exponents (거듭제곱과 지수)

### What You’ll Learn

* Understand what exponents mean  
* Learn how to evaluate expressions with powers  

### Key Ideas

* A power is a number written using a base and an exponent.  
* The exponent tells how many times to multiply the base.  
* Example format: $2^3 = 2 × 2 × 2 = 8$  

**Example:**

* $5^2 = 5 × 5 = 25$  
* $3^4 = 3 × 3 × 3 × 3 = 81$  

### Did You Know?

Exponents help us write big or repeated numbers more simply. Computers use powers of 2 in almost everything!

### Tips for Students

* The base is the number being multiplied.  
* The exponent tells **how many times** to multiply.  
* Read $2^3$ as “2 to the 3rd power” or “2 cubed.”

## 7.2 Square Roots and Cube Roots (제곱근과 세제곱근)

### What You’ll Learn

* Find square roots and cube roots  
* Understand how they relate to powers  

### Key Ideas

* A square root "undoes" squaring: $\sqrt{25} = 5$ because $5^2 = 25$  
* A cube root "undoes" cubing: $\sqrt[3]{8} = 2$ because $2^3 = 8$  
* The symbol $\sqrt{\phantom{x}}$ means square root  

**Example:**

* $\sqrt{36} = 6$  
* $\sqrt[3]{27} = 3$  
* $\sqrt{49} = 7$  

### Did You Know?

The ancient Babylonians could estimate square roots over 4,000 years ago—long before calculators!

### Tips for Students

* Memorize perfect squares (like 1, 4, 9, 16, 25…)  
* Cube roots are less common—focus on small ones like 1, 8, 27, 64  
* Use a calculator for harder roots if needed

## 7.3 Scientific Notation (과학적 표기법)

### What You’ll Learn

* Write very large or very small numbers using powers of 10  
* Convert between standard form and scientific notation  

### Key Ideas

* Scientific notation is a way to write numbers using powers of 10.  
* Format: $a × 10^n$ where $1 ≤ a < 10$ and $n$ is an integer  

**Example:**

* $3,000 = 3 × 10^3$  
* $0.0045 = 4.5 × 10^{-3}$  

### Did You Know?

Scientists use scientific notation to write distances in space or tiny measurements like bacteria size. It saves space and time!

### Tips for Students

* Move the decimal point to get a number between 1 and 10.  
* Count how many places you moved it—that’s your exponent.  
* Use positive exponents for big numbers, negative for small.
