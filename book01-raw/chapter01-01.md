## Numbers & Operations Overview

Welcome! In this book, you’ll explore the world of numbers and how we use them every day. You’ll learn about place value, decimals, fractions, and more. Each chapter includes explanations, examples, and practice problems to help you master these important math skills.

## Chapter 1: Place Value

### What is Place Value?

Place value tells us the value of each digit in a number, depending on its position. In our decimal system, each place is ten times the value of the place to its right.

**Example:**
- In \(4,275\):
  - 4 is in the thousands place (\(4,000\))
  - 2 is in the hundreds place (\(200\))
  - 7 is in the tens place (\(70\))
  - 5 is in the ones place (\(5\))

**Place Value Chart:**

| Place      | Value   |
|------------|---------|
| Thousands  | \(1,000\)   |
| Hundreds   | \(100\)     |
| Tens       | \(10\)      |
| Ones       | \(1\)       |

**Expanded Form Example:**  
\[
4,275 = 4,000 + 200 + 70 + 5
\]

Let's consider the number \(9,836\). The digit 8 is in the hundreds place, so its value is \(800\). This is because each digit's value depends on its position in the number. Similarly, if we write \(5,104\) in expanded form, we get \(5,000 + 100 + 0 + 4\). In the number \(12,345\), the digit 2 is in the thousands place, so it represents \(2,000\).

## Chapter 2: Decimals and Fractions

### What are Decimals and Fractions?

A **fraction** represents a part of a whole, like \(\frac{1}{2}\) or \(\frac{3}{4}\).  
A **decimal** is another way to write fractions, using a decimal point, like \(0.5\) or \(0.75\).

**Example:**  
\[
0.75 = \frac{75}{100} = \frac{3}{4}
\]

For instance, \(\frac{3}{10}\) as a decimal is \(0.3\). If you want to write \(0.6\) as a fraction in simplest form, you get \(\frac{3}{5}\), since \(0.6 = \frac{6}{10} = \frac{3}{5}\).

## Chapter 3: Comparing and Ordering Numbers

### How do we compare and order numbers?

We use symbols like \(>\) (greater than), \(<\) (less than), and \(=\) (equal to) to compare numbers. To order numbers, arrange them from least to greatest or greatest to least.

**Example:**
- Compare: \(0.7\ \_\_\ 0.65\) (Answer: \(>\))
- Order smallest to largest: \(\frac{3}{4},\ 0.5,\ 0.75,\ \frac{2}{3}\ \rightarrow\ 0.5,\ \frac{2}{3},\ \frac{3}{4},\ 0.75\)

Let's compare \(5.03\) and \(5.3\). Since \(5.03 < 5.3\), we use the less than symbol. Now, consider \(\frac{2}{5}\) and \(\frac{3}{7}\). Converting to decimals, \(\frac{2}{5} = 0.4\) and \(\frac{3}{7} \approx 0.428\), so \(0.4 < 0.428\), meaning \(\frac{2}{5} < \frac{3}{7}\). If we order the numbers \(0.2,\ \frac{1}{3},\ 0.25,\ 0.199\) from greatest to least, we get \(\frac{1}{3} (\approx 0.333),\ 0.25,\ 0.2,\ 0.199\). Comparing \(\frac{4}{9}\) and \(0.44\), we find \(\frac{4}{9} \approx 0.444\), so \(\frac{4}{9} > 0.44\).

## Chapter 4: Factors and Multiples

### What are Factors and Multiples?

A **factor** is a number that divides another number exactly with no remainder.  
A **multiple** is the product of a number and an integer.

**Example:**
- Factors of \(12\): \(\{1,\ 2,\ 3,\ 4,\ 6,\ 12\}\)
- Multiples of \(5\): \(5,\ 10,\ 15,\ 20,\ \ldots\)

For example, the factors of \(24\) are \(\{1,\ 2,\ 3,\ 4,\ 6,\ 8,\ 12,\ 24\}\). The first six multiples of \(8\) are \(8,\ 16,\ 24,\ 32,\ 40,\ 48\). If we ask whether \(9\) is a factor of \(81\), we see that \(81 \div 9 = 9\) with no remainder, so yes, \(9\) is a factor of \(81\).

## Chapter 5: Prime and Composite Numbers

### What are Prime and Composite Numbers?

A **prime number** has exactly two distinct factors: \(1\) and itself.  
A **composite number** has more than two factors.  
\(1\) is neither prime nor composite.

**Example:**
- Primes under \(20\): \(2,\ 3,\ 5,\ 7,\ 11,\ 13,\ 17,\ 19\)
- \(15\) is composite (factors: \(1,\ 3,\ 5,\ 15\))

Let's look at the numbers \(14,\ 17,\ 21,\ 23,\ 25\). Among these, \(17\) and \(23\) are prime because they have only two factors: 1 and themselves. The others are composite. All composite numbers between \(10\) and \(20\) are \(10,\ 12,\ 14,\ 15,\ 16,\ 18,\ 20\). The number \(1\) is neither prime nor composite because it has only one factor (itself), not two.

## Chapter 6: Greatest Common Factor & Least Common Multiple

### What are GCF and LCM?

The **Greatest Common Factor (GCF)** of two numbers is the largest factor they share.  
The **Least Common Multiple (LCM)** is the smallest non-zero multiple common to both.

**How to find them:**
- List prime factorizations and take common primes for GCF.
- For LCM, take all primes at highest powers.

**Example:**
- Find GCF and LCM of \(12\) and \(18\):
  - \(12 = 2^2 \times 3\); \(18 = 2 \times 3^2\)
  - \(\mathrm{GCF} = 2^1 \times 3^1 = 6\)
  - \(\mathrm{LCM} = 2^2 \times 3^2 = 36\)

For instance, the GCF of \(24\) and \(36\) is \(12\), since \(12\) is the largest number that divides both. The LCM of \(8\) and \(12\) is \(24\), the smallest number both divide into. For \(14\) and \(28\), we have \(14 = 2 \times 7\) and \(28 = 2^2 \times 7\), so the GCF is \(14\) and the LCM is \(28\).

## Did You Know?

- The word "digit" comes from the Latin word for finger, because people used their fingers to count.
- The decimal system is called "base-10" because each place is ten times the value of the place to its right.
- The largest single-digit number in the decimal system is \(9\).
- The introduction of zero by Indian mathematicians made calculations much clearer and easier.
